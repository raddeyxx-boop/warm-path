    One thing I’ve realized over the last 4–5 months is that AI-assisted engineering workflows are not something you “set up” once.
    They constantly evolve, as you go.
    As both a Software Engineer and Technical Project Manager, a big part of my time recently has gone into figuring out how to actually make these workflows useful in real-world engineering work instead of just generating impressive demos.
    That meant iterating on everything:
    feature planning,
    technical documentation,
    context engineering,
    sub-agent workflows,
    task decomposition,
    guardrails,
    handoff structures,
    review loops,
    and honestly… a lot of trial and error.
    Initial workflows often struggled with project complexity and agent integration. While some processes saved time, others created confusion.
    And the weirdest part is that the target keeps moving.
    New models show up.
    Tooling changes.
    Capabilities improve.
    Old workflows suddenly become outdated.

    Production systems really have a talent for making you question your life choices as a Software Engineer. 😭
    One moment you’re walking into Monday with coffee in hand.
    The next moment you’re staring at a “server down” email knowing the backend is gone.
    Monday blues just became purple.
    So the debugging started where it naturally should: The Backend.

    Started going through backend container logs, checking service health, restart behavior, deployment assumptions, the usual production detective work.
    That’s when Kafka started showing up in the logs.

    At that point the assumptions were fairly predictable:
    - networking,
    - service discovery,
    - deployment configs,
    - or Docker deciding it had suffered enough.

    So the debugging trail moved from the backend into the Kafka container itself, which eventually revealed that Kafka wasn’t actually failing to connect. It wasn’t starting at all.
    After tracing through container health, restart loops, and instance-level checks, the actual culprit underneath everything turned out to be:
    Disk exhaustion on the EC2 instance.
    Once storage was cleaned up and services restarted, Kafka recovered and the higher-level failures disappeared with it.
    The uncomfortable realization though was that this probably hadn’t happened overnight.

    Docker had quietly been accumulating image build up, unused layers, logs, deployment artifacts, cleanup assumptions left unaddressed until production eventually decided to file a complaint.

    The bigger lesson from incidents like this would be, most production failures are layered and the first error you see is often just a downstream symptom pretending to be the root cause; Restarts can temporarily hide problems.
    Tracing failures downward is usually what reveals the real issue.

    A few follow-ups came out of the incident afterward:
    - increased Kafka readiness windows
    - fixed deploy/rollback parity issues
    - improved cleanup handling
    - added plans for disk monitoring and retention policies

    A lot of debugging in real systems is honestly just separating the actual root cause from all the noisy secondary failures around it.

    I recently went down a rabbit hole trying to understand a past React Server Components (RSC) vulnerability that caused a lot of chaos across the ecosystem (including some Cloudflare mitigations).

    What clicked for me wasn't so much the bug itself but where it lived in the stack.

    》 In traditional client server apps, the client sends data, and the server decides behavior. Auth middleware, route guards, and validation all live at that boundary.
    》 With RSC and server actions, the model shifts a bit: the client sends serialized instructions, and the framework reconstructs server-side behavior from them.

    From what I understand, the vulnerability wasn't about missing auth or a careless developer. It was about a framework trusting client-sent serialized data too much during deserialization allowing unintended server-side code paths to execute below the app's auth layer.

    That led me to a rule of thumb I'm trying to internalize:
    👉 Whenever data crosses a boundary and becomes behavior, stop and get suspicious.
    This idea seems to apply far beyond React:
    ▪︎ RPC systems
    ▪︎ Unsafe deserialization plugin systems
    ▪︎ AI agents executing tool calls

    Different abstractions are powerful but they also shift trust boundaries in ways that aren't always obvious at first.

    (Side note: while reading up on this, I also realized that in newer versions of Next.js (15+), even dynamic route params are async a small thing, but a good reminder that framework internals evolve faster than our mental models.)

    I'm still learning and trying to build better intuition around these systems would love to hear how others think about trust boundaries in modern frameworks.

    Twenty years.
    Two decades of building, breaking, learning, unlearning—and rising again.
    That is the journey of     Indpro AB.

    Yes, the world around us kept shifting. Technologies evolved like seasons. Markets redefined themselves overnight. But beneath all that movement, our foundation held strong—because it was built with intention.
    Pavel, Tom , and Bobby didn’t just start a company.
    They planted a philosophy:

    Earn trust. Deliver world-class Swedish quality.
    Make it accessible. Keep it human.
    And it worked.

    With these principles in our pocket, we recently travelled across several companies in Bangalore with a Swedish team of entrepreneurs and tech geeks—the kind of people who can debate philosophy and algorithms with the same passion as they discuss coffee. After the whirlwind, we escaped to Goa to celebrate, unwind, and breathe for a moment.

    But of course, inspiration rarely takes vacations.

    Over dinner one evening, I turned to my colleague-turned-friend, Bhavya Bedi, and said:
    “Just imagine—almost everyone working around us right now, bringing this resort to life… could be replaced by machines in the coming years. What would that experience even feel like?”

    We both paused.
    Because neither of us had an answer ready.

    So I let the thought expand.
    A hotel with no humans—only quiet, efficient machines gliding through the corridors.
    Stores run entirely by automation.
    Malls, playgrounds, courts, resorts—industries once full of people, now humming with mechanical precision.

    A world where I step outside my home and see everything functioning perfectly…
    yet eerily empty of human fingerprints.

    No bad days.
    But no good days either.
    No spark.
    No emotion.
    No unexpected laughter from a stranger.
    No tired smile from someone who still shows up and tries.

    A world that works.
    But doesn’t feel.

    We often dream of perfect efficiency.
    But maybe it is our imperfections—our emotions, our struggles, our creativity—that make life meaningful.

    And as we stand at the edge of a technological era unlike any before, a question lingers:
    When everything becomes efficient,
    who will keep the world emotional?
    When everything becomes automated,
    who will keep it human?

    If you have made it to this line, I want to meet you in the comments.

    Because maybe the future belongs to those who dare to think about it—not with fear, but with imagination.
